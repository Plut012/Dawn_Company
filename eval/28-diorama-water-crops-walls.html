Eval 28 — Diorama: water, crops and walls

Dawn Company's diorama kit gets its own water, crops and wall tiles (src/client/render/kits/diorama/water.ts, crops.ts, walls.ts), and the board starts telling every tile what lies on its six neighbours. The works and the other units are still lowpoly's. Both boards below are the real createBoard, each with its own kit, showing the same PlayerView. Runs on Vite alone (npx vite --port 5199 --strictPort, then /eval/28-diorama-water-crops-walls.html).

4. Notes: what was judged, what was not, what falls short

Second pass: water that reads as water, straight wall runs, wheat ears. The stills above are the second pass's; the first pass's are kept as *-pass1.png and shown last.

  • Water. The surface now gives back the sky of the moment as a sheen, a colour keyed to the dawn like the rest of the air (blue-steel at 0, grey at 0.5, grey-gold at 1) and added to the surface as its own light, so it shows whatever the sun does. The camera never turns and has no perspective, so a true mirror would be one flat tone; the sheen is instead brighter towards the far (north) side of the board, as a sky's reflection is, which is what makes it read as a plane. Ripples are two scales of long faint streaks from the noise texture, in world space, seeded and still. Shallows take about half the sheen and mud none, so the banks still read. The values were set from the whole-board stills so that at each dawn the water is lighter than its banks and no brighter than open grass.
  • Walls. Each half of a wall hex's line is straight towards a side where the wall runs on to an east or west wall or house, and bowed towards a side where it ends, the two blended across the middle. A hex inside a run is straight from corner to corner (0.03 of sag); the last hex of a run is straight and then curls back behind the base; a lone hex is the bow. A straight wall stands across the back 0.15 of the base of a man in the hex, behind his feet. Spurs, turns down a hex's side and the lengths carried to a house are as they were.
  • Wheat. A stalk is now a leaf (one blade, seen from both sides), a stem and an ear: a lozenge a seventh of the stalk long, nodding, paler and yellower than the leaf. Stem and ear face the camera and have one face. Ripeness comes from two slow waves over the map plus a little per stalk, pushed to its ends, so there are green drifts and gold drifts across the field rather than one mixture. Stalks beside the clearing where a base goes lean away from it. Cost now: five triangles a stalk where it was four; counted on a hex inside a field, 2,770 triangles on the desktop setting (2,280 before) and 1,510 on the phone's (1,250). CROPS is unchanged: { desktop: { along: 0.05 }, phone: { along: 0.09 } }.

Judged from the stills. At board scale the stream now reads as water at dawn 0.5 and 1: a grey surface with streaks, darker banks and reeds, one run through the ford. At dawn 0 it is a dark blue surface with streaks under the mist; it reads as water where the mist is thin and is mostly mist where a bank lies on it. The north wall of the hamlet (F2, G2, H2) reads as one wall with a curl at each end. The field shows gold ears over green and a green drift through it (B3–C4 against A5–B6).

Not judged, second pass: a real phone and frame rate; the phone settings (counted only); a bigger stream or a pond (the sheen's north-south gradient is over the whole board, so a short east-west stream is one tone); a long wall run on a real map; whether the sheen is ever brighter than sunlit grass at other camera positions (it is set by eye from these stills, not measured).

Still short, second pass.

  • The sheen is a painted gradient, not a reflection: nothing on the bank is mirrored in the water, and the kit's rendered sky still adds almost nothing.
  • At dawn 0 the water under a bank of mist is hard to tell from low ground under mist, except by the label and the banks.
  • A straight wall takes the back of the base of the man in its hex. His feet, mark and most of the base are clear, but the base is cut.
  • The comb is still there: the lengths that go down to each house between the wall hexes north of it.
  • Ears are flat lozenges; close in the field is still plainly triangles, now of three kinds.

First pass.

Neighbours. tile(terrain, elevation, hex, around?): around is the six neighbours in neighbors() order (E, NE, NW, W, SW, SE), each { terrain, elevation } or null where there is no hex. The board now keeps what it has drawn on each hex in a small module, src/client/render/tiling.ts (createTiling(kit)), which asks the kit for every hex of a map with its neighbours and, when a hex's terrain changes (a work, in playback or in a new view), asks again for that hex and for each of its neighbours on the map; the board puts each new tile where the old one was and keeps its veil's state and whoever stands on it, as retile did for the one hex. The neighbours are taken from what is drawn, which in playback is ahead of the last view's map. Lowpoly ignores the argument: stills of the lowpoly boards of eval 26 and eval 27 taken now are byte for byte the committed ones.

How they are made.

  • Water. A hex prism whose top is 0.14 below the ground's, in its own material: dark, smooth (roughness 0.07 in the open), reflecting the small sky the kit already renders, drawn from world position so that water hexes run together without a seam. The land beside it shows its earth side as a bank by itself. The prism's top carries a number per rim point from around (1 across an edge with land beyond, 0 where water runs on or the board ends): the shader turns it into shallows, murky and less smooth, and mud at the very edge. Along each bank, three to nine clumps of reed and sometimes a stone (one geometry, about 300 triangles with banks all round; REEDS = { desktop: 9, phone: 5 }). The water takes only 30% of the haze by height, or it would be a grey hole; the mist's sheets lie over it as over all low ground.
  • Crops. The rows are the map's: one direction (18 degrees off east-west) and one spacing (0.105), in world space, and every stalk's place and colour come from where it is on the map, so hexes of crops are one field, ripening in drifts. The ground under it is the ground material's tilled soil in the same rows. Towards a neighbour that is not crops the stalks stop 0.08 to 0.2 short and the ground goes to trodden earth and turf: a headland. A stalk is a tuft of two crossed blades (four triangles), about 570 a hex (CROPS = { desktop: { along: 0.05 }, phone: { along: 0.09 } }: how far apart along a row; about 2,300 triangles a hex, 1,250 on the phone setting). A man in the corn: the tile is two geometries. The field is all that may stand whoever is there: the rim, to his knee and waist, cut low to the south so nothing is in front of his base or mark. The middle is the corn where his base goes and the tall corn to the south; the kit's occupied hook (27) hides it while a unit the board shows is in the hex. So an empty field is whole, and so is one with an enemy the player does not see in it (B3 here): the corn gives nothing away. The label lies on top of the corn, moved south by what its height shifts it north on screen, so it reads where every label does; it goes with the middle.
  • Walls. Dry stone: two rough courses and a coping of stones on edge, about half of them mossed, 0.2 to 0.24 high (boxes knocked out of square, painted in vertex colours; 750 to 1,700 triangles, one draw call). The line is a shallow bow across the north of the hex, behind the base. Walls join at the hexes' corners: to an east or west neighbour the bow runs out to that corner, where the neighbour's bow begins; to a north-east or north-west one a spur goes up to the north corner; to a south-east or south-west one the wall turns down the hex's side to the lower corner, where that neighbour's spur comes up. (The first attempt ran round a ring to each edge's middle: a row of wall hexes came out as a row of round pens. It was thrown away.) Towards a building the last length is carried 0.13 to 0.35 further, onto the house's own wall. The west side of a hex is drawn by the hex to the west if that is a wall, so no length is built twice.
  • The veil. All three are opaque, so the board's veil copies them whole (A3 crops, C7 water, I3 wall). A crops veil is copied with the tile told unoccupied, as a house's is.

Judged in the stills (headless Chromium, software WebGL, device pixel ratio 1): the stream reads as one dark water with banks, the ford as a way across; at dawn 0 mist lies on it. The ten crops hexes read as one field with an edge; the men at A4 and C5 stand in it with bases and marks plain; B3 is whole. The walls ring the two houses, joined, with the gaps at F4 and H4, and meet the houses' walls. The man at G2 stands in front of his wall with base and mark plain. After the round the walker is at the wall at C3, pinned, and the corn has closed behind him.

Not judged. A real phone and frame rate (a big field is the cost: 30 crops hexes are about 70,000 triangles on the desktop setting, none of them in the shadow pass); the phone settings (counted, not seen); the corn opening and closing in motion; a work going up next to a wall or a field in a real match (the neighbours' re-asking is tested through createTiling with a recording kit, and the board's use of it only by this page running); water, crops and walls on levels 1 and 2 (built by the tests, no still); a real map (crossroads) with the kit.

Where it falls short.

  • The reflection is too faint to see. The water is dark and has a sheen close in, but it does not visibly mirror the sky, and at dawn 1 a stream is nearly black from the board's distance. No current, no ripple (the board draws on demand).
  • The corn is spikes close in. At board scale it reads as a field; at three times closer each stalk is plainly a triangle, with no ears. It is gold-green all over rather than green going to gold in a way one would notice.
  • A man in the corn stands in a round clearing the size of his base, not among stalks to his waist: the base is 1.3 across in a hex 1.7 across. The corn round the clearing is to his knee and waist, and that is all the "in the corn" there is.
  • The wall's wave. A straight map wall is a row of shallow bows, and where it turns south it runs down a hex's side, so a ring is all corners. The lengths that go down to a house (between F2 and G2, G2 and H2 here) make the north wall look like a comb.
  • The wall's label is 0.3 further south than other hexes', and under a man's base when one stands there.
  • Every hex is re-asked when a neighbour changes, whether or not its tile would differ: seven tiles rebuilt for one work.

Deviations from the task doc. (1) The wall does not run from near the hex's centre to each neighbour: it bows across the north and joins at corners, for the reason above. (2) The corn's middle uses the occupied hook, which the doc does not ask for: without it every crops hex would have a permanent clearing. (3) The label of a crops hex floats on the corn; a wall hex's is moved south. (4) Off the board counts as more field and more water (no headland, no bank at the map's edge), and as no wall. (5) The board's tile bookkeeping moved into tiling.ts so that it can be tested without WebGL; index.ts keeps the layers, veils and occupied state.
